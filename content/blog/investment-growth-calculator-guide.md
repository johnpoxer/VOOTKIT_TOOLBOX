An investment calculator describes what could happen if a set of assumptions remained true. It cannot predict market returns, but it can show how starting balance, regular contributions, time, fees and return assumptions interact.

## Build the base projection

Open the [Investment Growth Calculator](/tools/finance/investment-calculator/) and enter the current portfolio value, monthly contribution, expected annual return and investment period. Use a return after estimated ongoing fees where possible.

The result combines growth on the starting balance with the future value of contributions. Deposits made early have more time in the model; later deposits contribute for fewer periods.

## Use a range, not one attractive rate

Create a cautious, middle and optimistic scenario. Then add a stress case with a lower return or several missed contribution months. This is more informative than changing multiple inputs until the calculator displays a desired target.

Historical averages are not guaranteed and depend on asset mix, period, reinvestment, fees and inflation. Actual markets rise and fall; a smooth projected curve is a mathematical illustration rather than the likely path of account statements.

## Separate contributions from growth

Look at how much of the final figure comes from money deposited and how much comes from projected returns. This prevents a common mistake: crediting the investment strategy for money that was actually saved from income.

If the goal has a fixed amount and deadline, use the [Savings Goal Planner](/tools/finance/savings-goal/) to calculate the required contribution. For understanding compounding frequency, see the [Compound Interest Calculator](/tools/finance/compound-interest/).

## Account for fees, taxes and inflation

A one-percentage-point annual fee difference can compound into a meaningful long-term gap. Taxes may apply to distributions, gains or withdrawals, and account wrappers differ by country. Inflation reduces the purchasing power of the final nominal balance.

The calculator cannot determine which investments suit your risk capacity or timeline. Money needed in the near future may not have time to recover from market declines. Diversification can reduce concentration but cannot eliminate loss.

## Questions people ask

### What return should I assume?

Use a range appropriate to the assets and subtract estimated fees. Include a lower case; do not select a rate merely because it reaches the target.

### Does monthly contribution timing matter?

Yes. Beginning- and end-of-period deposits have slightly different compounding time. Real contribution dates will not perfectly match a simplified model.

### Is this the same as compound interest?

The mathematics can use compounding, but investments have uncertain returns while a deposit product may state a contractual rate subject to its terms.

### Can the projection tell me what to buy?

No. It compares numerical scenarios and does not evaluate securities, risk tolerance, liquidity, taxes or suitability.

Investments can fall in value and past performance does not guarantee future results. This guide is educational, not investment advice.
