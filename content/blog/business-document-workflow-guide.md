A client project can become confusing even when the work itself is straightforward. The proposal describes one scope, a revised estimate uses another amount and the invoice arrives without a clear connection to either.

Use a consistent project name and reference across your working documents. Keep scope, pricing and payment status separate so the client can tell what has been proposed, what has been billed and what has actually been paid.

## Start with an explicit scope

Use [Proposal Generator](/tools/business/proposal-generator/) to organise the problem, deliverables and approach. Replace template text with the actual work. State what you will deliver, what the client must provide and how revisions will be handled.

For a fictional website project, “three-page brochure website using supplied copy and images” is more specific than “complete website”. Add assumptions about the content and the acceptance process in language both sides understand.

A generated proposal is a document draft. It does not verify that the scope is feasible, form an agreement automatically or replace any professional review your situation requires.

## Estimate effort before entering a price

[Hourly Rate Calculator](/tools/business/hourly-rate/) can help inspect the assumptions behind a rate. Separate billable project time from administration, learning and other work that does not appear directly on an invoice.

Use fictional numbers to check the arithmetic first. Twelve hours at 40 currency units per hour produces 480 before any separately agreed costs, discounts or taxes. Keep the chosen currency consistent across the proposal and the quote.

[Profit Margin Calculator](/tools/business/profit-margin/) helps distinguish revenue from cost. A price can cover the visible task while still overlooking support time or external expenses. The calculator only knows the costs you enter.

These tools provide arithmetic, not market-price recommendations or a guarantee that a project will be profitable.

## Create a quote that matches the proposal

Use [Quote & Estimate](/tools/business/quote-generator/) to list the deliverables and prices. Confirm quantities, currency, validity period and any assumptions about external costs. Where you describe an amount as an estimate, make that meaning clear in the accompanying wording.

Check whether each line corresponds to the scope. A client should not need to guess whether design, setup or handover is included. If the scope changes, produce an identifiable revision instead of silently replacing the earlier numbers.

Keep the earlier version for context and mark which version is current. A filename such as `project-quote-v2` is more useful than several files called `final`.

## Treat contract templates as a starting point

[Contract Generator](/tools/business/contract-generator/) can help assemble a template, but it does not know the applicable legal requirements or the full commercial relationship. Do not assume generated wording is appropriate for a regulated, high-value or unusual arrangement.

Verify the parties, scope, payment schedule, ownership provisions and dispute terms through an appropriate review process. Avoid promising legal enforceability in the document simply because it was generated as a PDF.

This guide describes an organisational workflow, not advice on which contractual terms you should accept.

## Build the invoice from the agreed version

Open [Invoice Generator](/tools/business/invoice-generator/) and use the agreed scope and billing event as the source. Check the client details, invoice reference, dates, line items and payment instructions.

In Vootkit's current calculation, a discount reduces the subtotal before the entered tax rate is applied. For an arithmetic example only, a subtotal of 500 with a 10% discount becomes 450; applying an illustrative 20% rate adds 90, producing 540.

That example does not establish which taxes apply to your transaction. Use the actual requirements for your business and location, and have uncertain treatment checked appropriately.

Read the [invoice walkthrough](/blog/create-professional-invoice/) for the document steps. Open the export before sending it and inspect long descriptions, totals and account references.

## Record payment before issuing a receipt

[Receipt Generator](/tools/business/receipt-generator/) prepares a payment record. Enter the amount and status from a verified payment record rather than assuming that creating an invoice means payment has occurred.

For part payments, make the amount received and any remaining amount clear in your records. Avoid a receipt that makes a partial payment look like full settlement.

The generator does not inspect your bank account, collect money or reconcile payments automatically. Keep supporting records in your existing bookkeeping system.

## Store a coherent handover pack

Keep the approved proposal, current quote, invoice, payment record and delivered work under the same project reference. Share only the files the recipient needs. Use [PDF tools](/tools/pdf/) if a document needs a checked export or a permitted combined pack.

The manual business sequence is separate from automated file processing. Review [workflow templates](/workflows/) for supported file operations, but do not assume they send invoices or manage a client account.

Before closing the project, compare the invoice with the approved quote and check any documented changes. Confirm the delivered files open correctly and that the recipient knows which version to use.

### Is an invoice the same as a receipt?

They serve different recordkeeping purposes: one records an amount billed, while the other records a payment. Keep the actual status clear.

### Does generating a document send it to the client?

No. Review and deliver the exported document through your chosen channel.

### What should I check most carefully?

Scope, currency, dates, totals and payment details. A polished layout cannot compensate for an incorrect amount or recipient.
