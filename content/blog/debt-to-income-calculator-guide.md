Debt-to-income ratio, usually shortened to DTI, compares recurring monthly debt obligations with gross monthly income. Lenders use it as one affordability signal, but it is not a complete household budget and it does not guarantee approval.

## Front-end and back-end DTI

Open the [Debt-to-Income Calculator](/tools/finance/debt-to-income/) and enter gross monthly income, housing costs and recurring debt payments.

**Front-end DTI = monthly housing obligations ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

**Back-end DTI = housing plus other recurring debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

The back-end ratio is broader. It can include car loans, minimum credit-card payments, student loans, personal loans, court-ordered obligations and other payments defined by the lender.

## Use gross income consistently

Gross income is income before tax and payroll deductions. Employees can usually begin with payslips or an employment contract, while variable, self-employed or commission income may be averaged under lender-specific rules. Do not simply use the strongest recent month.

DTI uses contractual debt payments, not every household expense. Groceries, utilities, transport, childcare and insurance can still determine whether a payment is affordable even when they do not appear in the lender's ratio. That is why DTI should be checked alongside the [50/30/20 Budget Calculator](/tools/finance/budget-calculator/).

## What ratio is considered acceptable?

You may see thresholds such as 36% or 43%, but they are conventions, not universal limits or personal targets. Standards vary by country, lender, loan programme, credit profile, deposit, reserves and property type. A lower ratio can still be unaffordable when essential expenses are high; a higher ratio may be accepted under particular rules.

Read our broader [debt-to-income ratio guide](/blog/debt-to-income-ratio/) for the distinction between lender screening and everyday affordability.

## Ways to test the calculation

Run the ratio using current obligations. Then remove a debt only if it will genuinely be repaid before the application. Finally, test the proposed new payment. This shows whether paying down a monthly obligation changes capacity more than adding the same cash to a deposit.

Do not omit a debt because it is inconvenient or absent from one statement. The lender may verify credit files, bank records and legal obligations.

## Questions people ask

### Does rent count as debt?

Treatment varies. Housing expense is relevant to front-end affordability, while a lender's formal back-end calculation follows its programme rules.

### Should credit cards use the balance or minimum payment?

DTI generally uses a required monthly payment, but lenders may calculate one when the report does not show it. Follow the lender's definition.

### Does DTI affect credit score?

DTI itself is not normally a credit-score component, but debts and payment history can affect credit reports and lending decisions.

### Can a calculator predict approval?

No. Income verification, credit, collateral, deposit, reserves and lender policy also matter.

This is an educational estimate, not lending advice. Use the applicant criteria supplied by the relevant lender for an official assessment.
