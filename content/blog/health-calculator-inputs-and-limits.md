A calculator turns selected inputs into a result using a defined rule. It does not examine you, measure every relevant variable or decide whether a result is appropriate for your circumstances. Health-related numbers need that distinction especially clearly.

Use Vootkit's health tools to understand the arithmetic and prepare questions. The examples below are fictional calculations, not personal targets, diagnoses or treatment instructions.

## Check units before interpreting the result

Write down the measurement, unit and date. Entering 180 centimetres as 180 inches produces a very different result even though both fields contain an ordinary-looking number.

Use [Unit Converter](/tools/everyday/unit-converter/) if your measurements come from different sources. Do not switch between rounded imperial and metric values repeatedly; unnecessary rounding can change the final display.

Also check whether a field expects total minutes, a time in hours and minutes, or a decimal value. Thirty minutes is half an hour, not 0.30 hours. Unit mistakes can look like surprising health findings when they are simply input errors.

## BMI describes weight relative to height

[BMI Calculator](/tools/health/bmi-calculator/) uses weight divided by height squared. A fictional adult weighing 80 kilograms at 1.80 metres has a BMI of about 24.7: `80 ÷ (1.8 × 1.8)`.

That arithmetic does not measure body fat directly or establish an individual's overall health. The [CDC describes BMI as a screening measure](https://www.cdc.gov/bmi/adult-calculator/bmi-categories.html) that should be considered alongside other factors. Its adult categories apply from age 20; children and teenagers require different interpretation.

Do not use a single category label as a diagnosis or a judgement about a person. If you have questions about what a result means for you, discuss them with a qualified clinician.

## Separate resting energy from activity estimates

[BMR Calculator](/tools/health/bmr-calculator/) uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation in the current implementation and then applies a selected activity multiplier to estimate total daily energy expenditure.

These are two distinct steps. The equation estimates resting energy from the supplied characteristics; the multiplier adds another assumption about activity. Neither step measures actual energy expenditure.

For a purely mathematical example, an estimated resting value of 1,600 multiplied by 1.2 gives 1,920, while the same value multiplied by 1.55 gives 2,480. The 560 difference comes entirely from the selected factor. It is not evidence that a person's metabolism changed between clicks.

Record the chosen factor when saving the result. Do not treat the displayed value as a prescribed intake or assume that the interface's permitted age range establishes clinical suitability.

## Macro percentages are shares of energy

[Macro Calculator](/tools/health/macro-calculator/) converts a supplied calorie amount and selected split into grams using its 4/4/9 arithmetic for carbohydrate, protein and fat.

For example, 25% of a hypothetical 2,000-calorie input is 500 calories. Dividing 500 by four gives 125 grams for a four-calorie-per-gram component; dividing by nine gives about 55.6 grams for fat. Equal percentages of energy do not imply equal weights.

The example explains the conversion, not which split anyone should choose. A preset name is a software option, not a personalised nutritional recommendation. The tool does not assess medical history or dietary adequacy.

## Body measurements are method-dependent

[Body Fat Calculator](/tools/health/body-fat-calculator/) estimates from supplied measurements. Read the measurement instructions carefully and record where and how each circumference was taken.

[Ideal Weight Calculator](/tools/health/ideal-weight-calculator/) displays formula-based estimates. Different formulas can disagree because their assumptions differ. The word “ideal” in the tool name should not be read as a universal personal goal.

When comparing results, keep the method, units and measurement conditions consistent. A changed tape position or a different equation can produce a different number without establishing a meaningful physical change.

## Hydration and heart-rate outputs need context

[Water Intake Calculator](/tools/health/water-intake-calculator/) produces an estimate from limited inputs. It cannot account for every circumstance or an individually prescribed fluid plan. A calculated number should not override advice you have been given for a medical condition.

[Heart Rate Calculator](/tools/health/heart-rate-calculator/) similarly uses equations and supplied inputs. It does not measure your maximum heart rate or perform an exercise assessment. Do not read its displayed zones as instructions to push through symptoms or as a substitute for individual guidance.

Keep the assumptions visible when discussing either output. A rounded estimate with a clear explanation is more useful than an apparently precise number detached from its method.

## Pace is a useful arithmetic cross-check

[Pace Calculator](/tools/health/pace-calculator/) relates distance, time and pace. Covering five kilometres in 30 minutes corresponds to six minutes per kilometre. At the same pace, ten kilometres takes 60 minutes mathematically.

That calculation does not predict whether a person can sustain the pace over the longer distance. Terrain, conditions and individual capacity are outside the arithmetic.

### Which result should I trust when calculators disagree?

Compare the inputs, units and equations first. A different method can legitimately produce a different estimate; clinical interpretation requires more context.

### Does more decimal precision mean more accuracy?

No. The uncertainty in the measurements and assumptions can be much larger than the final decimal place.

### What should I take to an appointment?

If a result concerns you, bring the inputs, date, method and the question you want answered. That is more informative than an isolated screenshot of a score.
